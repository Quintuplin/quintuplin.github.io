Q1 How far does the analogy of classic TDD with greedy pathing or gradient descent hold, and do variants that change the map, the step size, the breadth of search or the check reach a better end point?

Status: answered

Classic TDD writes the smallest failing test and then makes the smallest change that passes it, which resembles greedy pathing or gradient descent toward the requirements.

The analogy holds for the climb and no variant reached a better end point. All 10 variants and Classic greedy TDD reached the same functional end point of 15/15 with 76/76 hidden and 69/69 unseen tests. No LOG.md row of the 14 full-1 clones lowered the hidden pass count, so no method stepped back.

The methods differed in how early tests passed per weighted token. At 500000 weighted tokens held-out passed 0.42 of the hidden tests, coarse-to-fine and mutation-guided 0.39, classic 0.29, annealed 0.26 and tpp 0.05. Noun-verb, functions-first, mapped-path, property-based and multi-start passed none.

At 750000 noun-verb, functions-first and mutation-guided passed all 76 tests and classic and held-out 0.68. Mapped-path, property-based and multi-start passed 0.03 or less. In rep-2, a different batch from the survey, the median share passed at 500000 weighted tokens over 5 replicates was 0.00 for held-out, 0.34 for coarse-to-fine, 0.09 for mutation-guided and 0.29 for classic, a descriptive figure with no test.

Multi-start's 3 start clones alone cost 869732 weighted tokens before its final clone began. Later commits deleted 33 of the 147 src lines classic added, a rewrite share of 22 percent. Functions-first rewrote 43 percent, annealed 35 and tpp 34.

The other 7 methods rewrote 11 percent or less, with none for held-out and multi-start. So the small steps of classic did not lead to the largest rewrite. Each survey method ran once on one task, so the survey figures are descriptive and a gap among them may be run noise.

Q2 Can each TDD methodology be written as a short numbered procedure that an AI coding agent carries out unattended from start to finish?

Status: answered

Yes for all 11.

Each procedure has 7 or 8 steps and every method reached a final clone with no failed collection step. The harness turned minute timeboxes into cycle caps and throwaway branches into uncommitted trials. Rule-only steps got no agent of their own.

Two calls hit the 15-cycle cap.

Q5 Were the hidden grading tests kept away from every implementing agent, so that the hidden-test score is a fair measure?

Status: answered

Yes as far as git access goes.

Each clone came from the starter tag with --no-local and then lost its origin remote, so main and its hidden suite were not reachable through git. Protection was prompt plus audit with no settings deny. The 10 high audit flags were 9 on Held-out TDD and 1 on Noun-verb map, and each was judged a false positive. rep-2 raised 50 high flags in 9 of its 20 builds.

46 are on the 5 Held-out TDD replicates, 37 for added code that uses reflection and 9 for _heldout paths. The other 4 are outside-path flags on rep-2-r02, rep-2-r10, rep-2-r13 and rep-2-r09. 49 were judged false positives.

The rep-2-r09 flag is a minor true positive. That build ran ls ../ from its clone, which listed only the names in the clones dir, _heldout among them, and read no file outside its clone.

Q12 Beyond functional correctness, how do the implementations compare in test strength (mutation score) and in reviewed code quality and alterability?

Status: partly answered

Mutation scores run from 75.2 percent for held-out at 112/149 to 94.7 percent for mapped-path at 107/113.

A seeded permutation chi-square over the 11 methods gives chi-square 63.0 and p below 0.001. Fisher exact tests with Holm correction separate 11 of the 55 pairs at 0.05. Each of those pairs includes held-out or noun-verb.

The hidden suite also passes 77 of the 164 surviving mutants, so those are likely equivalent mutants or gaps in the hidden suite. The adjusted score drops them and runs from 79.4 percent for held-out at 112/141 to 100 percent for classic, coarse-to-fine, mutation-guided and property-based. Holm then separates 22 of the 55 pairs.

The tests treat each mutant as independent within one run per method. The mutant counts differ by method, so the scores compare only loosely. The mutation figures are recomputed.

Code quality comes from a blind rating by three raters per final on the eight cqp.html principles. Ordinal alpha over the 11 finals reads, as principle, alpha and status: Clear Presentation 0.62 unchecked, Explanatory Language 0.41 unchecked, Consistent Code not measured unchecked, Used Content 0.58 unchecked, Simple Constructs 1.00 accepted, Minimal Duplication 0.88 accepted, Modular Structure 0.85 accepted and Problem Alignment 0.75 tentative. Consistent Code has no alpha because all 33 of its ratings are 4.

Simple Constructs rests on one varying item. Every rater gave classic 3 and every other final 4, so 34.7% of resamples hold no variation and its interval is not estimable. The headline shows the medians of the accepted and tentative principles.

Each of those medians is 3, 4 or 5. The Methods table shows the medians of all eight principles. The Column definitions tag the unchecked ones unchecked.

The alphas and medians are recomputed. Alterability and test quality are not measured. The rating leaves them out and the single review per method that scored them is superseded.

In rep-1 the median adjusted mutation over 5 replicates is 0.927 for functions-first, 0.964 for noun-verb, 0.984 for classic and 1.000 for anchor-engineering. A separate blind rating of the 20 rep-1 finals accepts Clear Presentation and Problem Alignment and takes Used Content and Modular Structure as tentative. Its median CQP over the four counted principles is 14 for classic, 15 for functions-first and 16 for noun-verb and anchor-engineering.

In rep-2 the median adjusted mutation over 5 replicates is 0.777 for held-out and 1.000 for classic, coarse-to-fine and mutation-guided. The blind rating of the 20 rep-2 finals accepts no principle and takes Minimal Duplication, Modular Structure and Problem Alignment as tentative.

Q13 Does test-driving pure functions first and grouping them into classes later give a better structure than planning the classes first from the nouns and verbs of the specification?

Status: partly answered

The functions-first method gave 6 classes, 45 methods and 270 source lines.

It passed strict PMD and cost 1273066 weighted tokens. The noun-verb method gave 14 classes, 60 methods and 378 source lines. It passed strict PMD and cost 961206 weighted tokens.

The mean cost to pass a hidden test is M 470362 and M_io 441270 for functions-first against M 545018 and M_io 415798 for noun-verb, so leaving output tokens out reverses the order. Functions-first scores 117/132 on mutation against 88/115 for noun-verb. Fisher exact gives p 0.017 for that pair and 0.59 after Holm over all 55 pairs.

The adjusted scores are 117/122 against 88/109 with p 0.0003 and 0.010 after Holm. These tests compare two programs. Each method ran once.

On the accepted and tentative rating principles both have median 4, except Modular Structure at 4 for functions-first and 3 for noun-verb. One median point on one program each shows neither structure better. The planned rep-1 contrast repeats the pair with 5 replicates each in one batch.

Median adjusted mutation is 0.927 for functions-first and 0.964 for noun-verb, with Mann-Whitney U 16 and Holm p 0.548. The CQP sensitivity check gives Holm p 0.333. So the replicates show neither structure better on test strength or rated quality.

Q14 Does each methodology approach full correctness gradually, as gradient descent would, or in one jump?

Status: answered

Both shapes occur.

A curve counts as one jump when one step holds half or more of its gain. That rule was fixed before the shapes were read. Of the 14 full-1 clones 7 rose in one jump and 7 gradually.

Noun-verb, functions-first, multi-start and its 3 start clones went from 0 to 76/76 hidden tests in one LOG.md row. Property-based gained 52 of its 76 in one row. The 7 gradual clones gained on 4 to 9 rows with a largest step of 20 to 32 tests.

Five of them gained their last 24 tests in their final row. Cumulative weighted tokens give all full-1 methods a shared x axis. On it the largest step ended at 0.49 of the cost to completion for functions-first, 0.52 for held-out and 0.73 to 1.0 for the other clones.

Of the 5 tcp-1 arms 4 rose gradually over their 12 loop calls. The risk arm was one jump. It held at 38 tests for six calls and then gained the other 38 in one call.

No step of any of the 19 curves lost a hidden test. Each method ran once, so the classes are descriptive and any link to method family is post hoc.

Evidence: runs/full-1/curve-shapes.tsv; runs/tcp-1/*/curve.json p; harness/report/curve.py shape. recomputed

Q15 Can any difference between two methodologies be attributed to the methodology itself?

Status: partly answered

Partly.

In the survey each method ran once with one model on one task, so no significance test is possible and every survey gap is a single observation. rep-1 fixed two contrasts before any build and ran 5 replicates of each method in one batch. Anchor Engineering scored a higher adjusted mutation score than Classic greedy TDD with U 25 and Holm p 0.016, which ties that gap to the method for this task, model and batch. Functions first, classes later and Noun-verb map did not differ, Holm p 0.548. rep-2 fixed three contrasts before any build, each survey leader against Classic greedy TDD on M with 5 replicates each in one batch.

No leader differed from Classic greedy TDD on M, Holm p 1.000 for all three. On the secondary adjusted mutation score Held-out TDD scored lower than Classic greedy TDD with Holm p 0.024. Costs of full-1 and rep-1 do not compare, as the Limits explain.

Partly.

All 426 full-1 src edits the audit found and 88 of the 92 tcp-1 src edits it found used the Write or Edit tools. No Bash call the audit recognises wrote src and kept the change. The other 4 tcp-1 edits were reverted fault injections.

Each was a Bash sed that broke src for one test run, and the same call undid it with git restore. The check does not detect git apply, patch or script files such as sh .scratch/x.sh. Of 198 full-1 test edits followed by a src edit 162 had a red run of the edited test class between them.

15 had no test run. 21 are unclear. In 10 of those only passing runs came between.

In 11 no red run named an edited test class. 7 of those followed a spec-file edit. A spec-file edit names no class, so its red runs stay unclear.

Of 42 such tcp-1 edits 20 had a red run of the edited test class, 4 had no test run and 18 are unclear. Of 109 full-1 LOG.md rows 3 claim a test count that differs from the net test methods added since the row before.

Each method ran once. The next two claims come from findings.json and are unchecked. Each method's LOG.md has 0 to 9 rows that mention a conflict.

Two calls hit the cycle cap. The same audit over the 20 rep-1 replicates flagged 1 of 850 src edits as a Bash write, in a functions-first replicate. Of 222 rep-1 test edits followed by a src edit 175 had a red run of the edited test class, 25 were flagged and 22 are unclear, and 19 of the flagged are in functions-first.

Of 168 rep-2 test edits followed by a src edit 119 had a red run of the edited test class, 8 were flagged and 41 are unclear. 9 of 162 rep-2 LOG.md rows were flagged.

No rep-2 flag was sampled by hand, so their precision is unchecked.

Q17 Does the TDD literature lack a defined method for building and ordering the test list that drives development?

Status: partly answered

Partly.

The sources read give a few defined methods for building a test list and none for ordering one. Pratama et al. 2026 derive one test per basis path of a use case.

Riquelme et al. 2019 generate tests from models. Neither was tested beyond one hypothetical case or small tool trials.

Guerra et al. 2014 and the worked example in Beck 2002 treat the list as an informal checklist that grows during the session. Ordering rests on heuristics.

Guerra et al. 2013 name step types such as the simplest scenario first and small increments and give no ranking procedure. Martin's Transformation Priority Premise of 2013 is the most specific ordering rule found.

Its author calls the transformations informal at best and their order not likely correct. Beck 2023 calls picking the next test a skill that only comes with experience and says the order can significantly affect the result. In a 2011 Stack Overflow thread the accepted answer says there is no procedure to write the tests and the list.

Khan and Arshad 2012 found 7 test-list sources and all 7 are grey literature (p. 33). None of the sources read compares one list or order against another.

These claims are verified against the saved sources and their critiques. The answer stays partial for five reasons. ACM DL and Scopus could not be searched on 25 Oct 2026 and OpenAlex stood in.

Forward snowballing from Guerra 2013 and 2014 was not run. 23 full texts could not be obtained. The abstracts or snippets of 5 of those works describe building or ordering rules in adjacent settings.

They are unchecked and one could narrow the ordering claim. Beck 2002 was read through publisher excerpts only, so its Test List pattern and Red Bar Patterns chapter are unread. One reviewer screened all records with rule assistance.

Q18 Can regression test case prioritisation criteria (coverage, cost, risk and usage) be carried over to ordering a TDD test list before any of its tests exist?

Status: partly answered

Partly.

They can be carried over, and in tcp-1 they did not clearly help. The usage ratings matched simulated random play. The cost and risk ratings showed no detectable link to the measured data, and 12 candidates are too few to rule out a moderate one.

A lister agent wrote 19 requirement clauses and 12 candidate tests with the clauses each checks and with risk, usage and cost estimates. The list passed the harness checks with 0 audit flags. Each of the 5 arms built all 12 candidates in its order in 12 loop calls with 0 off-order calls and its list files intact.

Only the cost order scored above random on both APFD-I and APFDc-H. Usage scored above random on APFDc-H only. No criterion reached completion for fewer weighted tokens than random.

Q20 gives the figures. Each rating check below is a Spearman rho with a seeded permutation p against rho = 0 and a seeded percentile bootstrap 95% interval, each over 10000 draws at seed 20261024. All are recomputed.

Usage against the share of moves that exercise each clause gave rho 0.974, p 0.0001 and an interval of 0.80 to 1.00 over n = 9 clauses, R4, R7 to R11 and R12 to R14. A p of 0.0001 is the smallest 10000 draws can give. The shares come from 10000 seeded games of random legal moves split over the 6-4 and 7-5 boards.

They measure rule use under random play and not under human play. R12 to R14 share one end-of-game value. Taken as one point they give rho 0.991 at n = 7.

The cost estimate against src lines added per candidate gave rho 0.03, p 0.92 and an interval of -0.57 to 0.59 at n = 12. The risk of each candidate's riskiest checked clause against its red-green cycles gave rho 0.19, p 0.65 and an interval of -0.38 to 0.68 at n = 12. Both measures are means over the 5 arms.

Src lines depend on build order, since an early candidate adds code that later ones reuse. The cycles are the agents' own LOG.md counts. Every row logs 0 or 1, and 6 rows, all in the cost arm, log a cycle although the actions say the test never went red on behaviour.

At n = 12 a two-sided test at the 0.05 level has 80% power only for a true rho of about 0.73 or above by the Fisher z approximation, so a weaker link would likely go unseen. The gap was how far the ratings depend on the lister. A second blind lister in run tcp-1-list2 rated the same 19 clauses and wrote the checks of the same 12 candidates.

Its usage ratings agreed with the first lister's with rho 0.962 and an interval of 0.882 to 0.997 at n = 19. Its risk ratings agreed with weighted kappa 0.530 at n = 19, a moderate agreement. Each candidate's checked clauses overlapped the first list's with a mean Jaccard of 0.71.

So the usage ratings hold across two listers and the risk ratings only in part.

Q21 Should the approach in the post "Principles of Anchor Engineering" become an additional methodology arm?

Status: partly answered

Partly.

The post was saved and critiqued on 25 Oct 2026 and judged against four criteria for a new arm. It fails two, passes one and one is unclear. Whether to adopt it as an arm is open.

The post is a blog post with no peer review. It measures or compares nothing. It states five principles and puts human judgement in an Anchor Book kept in the repository.

The Book holds the design, anchors that state what must be true about the system, the check for each anchor and planned chunks of work. It fails the executable-steps criterion. It gives no numbered procedure, Book schema or anchor format and leaves the mechanics to later posts.

The one follow-up post of 10 Oct 2026 gives none either. Its judgement check-in and its conflict surfacing need a human. The harness tells agents never to stop to ask.

It passes the criterion of not relabelling noun-verb or mapped-path. An anchor is a property with its check and not a class or verb of their map. It fails the measurable-prediction criterion because it predicts none of the measured columns.

Its fit to the F and L stage kinds is unclear. An agent-only construction would fit. The human check-in matches no stage kind and replacing it would change the method.

Any arm built from the post would be the experimenters' own design. Its anchor list would partly restate the numbered clauses of the tcp list. An agent-only arm named Anchor Engineering was then written from the post and ran 5 replicates in rep-1.

All 5 reached adjusted mutation 1.000 and it scored higher than Classic greedy TDD with Holm p 0.016. The arm's steps remain the experimenters' own design, so the result speaks for that arm and not for the post's human-in-the-loop method.
